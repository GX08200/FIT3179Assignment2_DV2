Global Carbon Emissions

Beyond the Totals: Who Emits, Who Is Changing?

Does a small share of global emissions also mean low emissions per person?

Global CO₂ Emissions in 2024

Figure 1

China recorded the highest annual CO₂ emissions in 2024, followed by the United States in second place and India in third. This chart displays all 20 of the highest-emitting countries, making it easy to compare their annual totals and identify which countries emit the most and which emit relatively less within this group.

Figure 2

This treemap shows each country's share of global CO₂ emissions in 2024. China accounts for about 33%, the United States about 12%, and India about 8%. Together, these three countries contribute approximately 53% of global emissions, more than half of the world total, making them major emitters, with China ranking first. However, China has roughly 1.4 billion people, compared with around 340 million in the United States. Rankings based on total emissions may therefore differ from rankings based on emissions per person. The next section examines CO₂ emissions per capita.

Figure 3

This map reveals how CO₂ emissions per person vary across countries. Although China ranks first in total emissions, its per-capita emissions of 9.13 tonnes fall in the third of the five map categories (5 to below 10 tonnes per person per year). The United States has higher per-capita emissions, at 13.59 tonnes, placing it in the fourth category (10 to below 20 tonnes), alongside Canada, Australia and Russia. In Europe, Germany, Italy and Poland fall in the same category as China, as does Malaysia, at 8.57 tonnes per person. Several Gulf countries have particularly high per-capita emissions: Saudi Arabia (17.73 tonnes), Oman (17.89 tonnes) and the United Arab Emirates (19.45 tonnes) are in the fourth category, while Kuwait (25.51 tonnes) and Qatar (45.33 tonnes) are in the highest category (20 tonnes or more). Qatar therefore has one of the highest per-capita emissions figures in the world. Palau has the highest per-capita CO₂ emissions, at 63.73 tonnes per person per year.

CO₂ Emissions per Person over Time

Figure 4

The chart shows that CO₂ emissions per person in the United States remained higher than in China throughout 1970–2024. China started at a low level of about one tonne per person in 1970, followed by very slow growth. Growth accelerated after 2000, and per-capita emissions continued to rise overall, despite some fluctuations. The United States began at a much higher level in 1970 and followed an overall downward trend. The decline became more pronounced from around 2007, with emissions continuing to fall overall in the years that followed. These patterns may be linked to the broader historical context.

Figure 5

This waffle chart compares the composition of U.S. energy-related CO₂ emissions in 2007 and 2024. In 2007, electric power accounted for the largest share at 40.26%, followed by transportation at 33.60%. By 2024, transportation had become the largest contributor at 38.91%, while the share from electric power had fallen to 29.80%, a decrease of 10.46 percentage points. The industrial share increased from 16.81% to 19.95%, while residential and commercial emissions accounted for smaller shares in both years. Together, transportation and electric power represented about three-quarters of emissions in 2007 and just over two-thirds in 2024. These percentages show how emissions were distributed across sectors in each year; an increase in a sector’s share does not necessarily mean its absolute emissions increased.

Figure 6

This proportional symbol map shows how total fossil fuel consumption varied across the countries and territories with available data in 2025. China recorded the highest consumption at approximately 39,443 TWh, followed by the United States at 21,677 TWh, India at 10,100 TWh and Russia at 7,782 TWh. China consumed about 1.8 times as much fossil energy as the United States and nearly four times as much as India. The largest circles are concentrated in Asia and North America, while European countries generally have smaller individual totals. These values represent total consumption of coal, oil and gas, so they do not directly indicate consumption per person or the amount of CO₂ emitted.

Figure 7

This connected dot chart compares CO₂ emissions in 2023 and 2024 for the 20 highest-emitting countries in 2024. Emissions increased in 15 countries and decreased in five. China remained the largest emitter, with an increase of 103.50 million tonnes, while India recorded the largest absolute rise at 139.31 million tonnes, followed by China and Russia. Vietnam had the fastest percentage growth in this group at 10.26%, although its absolute increase of 40.09 million tonnes was smaller than India’s. Japan recorded the largest absolute reduction at 30.17 million tonnes, while the United Kingdom had the largest percentage decline at 4.06%. The enlarged change panel makes these annual differences easier to compare, showing that a country’s emissions ranking and its rate of change describe different aspects of its emissions.

U.S. Operating Power Plants (2025)

Each equal-sized dot represents one of 13,348 power plants with operating generating units in the 2025 EIA-860 inventory. Hover over a dot for plant details, or use the primary-energy selector below the map.

Coverage: 50 states and Washington, DC. Alaska and Hawaii appear as insets at different scales. Plants are counted once by EIA plant code, with at least one generator classified as operating (OP). Proposed, retired, standby and out-of-service units are excluded.

State boundaries: Natural Earth 1:50m Admin 1. Download power plant CSV.

Figure 8

This map connects the national carbon-emissions story to the energy infrastructure behind electricity supply. Figure 5 shows that electric power accounted for 29.80% of U.S. energy-related CO₂ emissions in 2024. The 2025 plant map adds geographic context by locating operating plants and distinguishing their primary energy sources. Solar, wind and hydroelectric plants greatly outnumber coal, natural-gas and petroleum plants in this inventory, highlighting the prominence of renewable-energy infrastructure and its potential to support environmental goals and the transition towards lower-carbon electricity. Wind plants are concentrated across the central states, while many hydroelectric plants are found in the Pacific Northwest, illustrating regional differences in the energy system. Fuel choice matters for carbon emissions: coal, natural gas and petroleum plants release CO₂ when fuels are burned, while wind, solar, hydro and nuclear power generate electricity without burning fossil fuels (EIA). These technologies provide the infrastructure through which electricity supply can shift towards lower-carbon sources, helping connect the national emissions trends above with the energy transition on the ground. Actual emissions reductions depend on electricity output and the fossil-fuel generation displaced, rather than plant counts alone.

Where Did U.S. Emissions Fall? (2007–2024)

Follow the change from the 2007 total to the 2024 total. Blue bars show sector reductions; orange shows an increase. Hover over each bar for its contribution.

Units: million tonnes of CO₂ (Mt). Totals sum the five rounded sector values used in Figure 5. Electricity-generation emissions belong to electric power and are not counted again in other sectors. Source: EIA Monthly Energy Review, May 2026, Tables 11.2–11.6. Download waterfall CSV.

Figure 9

U.S. energy-related CO₂ emissions fell from approximately 6,015 million tonnes in 2007 to 4,788 million tonnes in 2024, a reduction of 1,227 million tonnes, or 20.4%. Electric power contributed the largest reduction, at 995 million tonnes, equivalent to about 81% of the net decrease. Transportation, industry and residential emissions also declined, while commercial emissions increased slightly. Unlike the percentage shares in Figure 5, this waterfall shows changes in absolute emissions, identifying the sectors behind the national decline.

U.S. Electric Power CO₂ Emissions by Fuel (1973–2024)

The stacked areas show emissions from each fuel, and their combined height shows the electric power sector total. The dashed line marks 2007, the comparison year used in Figures 5 and 9.

Other reported sources are the sector total minus coal, natural gas and petroleum, including reported geothermal and non-biomass waste emissions. This chart measures CO₂ emissions, rather than electricity generated. Source: EIA Monthly Energy Review, May 2026, Table 11.6 (official Excel archive). Download fuel history CSV.

Figure 10

The decline in U.S. electric power emissions after 2007 was dominated by falling coal-related emissions. Coal emissions decreased from approximately 1,986 million tonnes in 2007 to 672 million tonnes in 2024, a fall of about 66%. Over the same period, natural-gas emissions increased from 371 to 735 million tonnes, partially offsetting that reduction. Total electric power emissions nevertheless fell by about 41%. Together with Figure 8, this chart links the geography of power infrastructure to changes in emissions over time, although emissions data alone cannot determine how much of the decline came from renewables, fuel switching, efficiency or changing electricity demand.

Large Totals, High Emissions per Person? (2024)

Each dot represents a country or territory. Compare total emissions horizontally and emissions per person vertically. The horizontal axis is logarithmic: equal spacing represents a tenfold increase. Type a country name below the chart to highlight it.

All 207 records with positive total and per-capita values are included. A logarithmic axis cannot include zero. Dot size is constant and does not encode population. Source: Worldometer, CO₂ emissions by country (2024). Download country CSV.

Figure 11

A large national emissions total does not necessarily mean the highest emissions per person. China had the largest total in 2024, at approximately 13,125 million tonnes, but its per-capita figure of 9.13 tonnes was below the United States at 13.59 tonnes. India was another major emitter by total, while recording only 2.19 tonnes per person. Conversely, Palau and Qatar had much smaller totals but high per-capita emissions of 63.73 and 45.33 tonnes respectively. This comparison returns to the dashboard’s central question: national totals and per-capita emissions reveal different aspects of the global carbon-emissions picture.
